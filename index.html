what it actually is

Merging is one part of it.

Your Claude, Codex and open-model subscriptions, on one repo, as one agent. antiloki routes each job of an ask to the model measured best for it — so the frontier seat is asked only for the parts that need it and your weekly limit goes further. It controls what may be touched, records every read, write and command as it happens, and measures the whole thing in seconds and dollars. The merge is what routing produces — the rest is why it can be trusted.

control

One agent, fenced where you say.

Decide which folders may be written. The gate sits at the tool call, outside the model — a change beyond the fence is refused, shown, and undone in one click.

verify

The record is a machine's.

Every read, write and command is logged by a hook outside the model, and every turn is priced and timed. That part cannot be talked out of. On top of it you switch on what you want proved — the project's own test, build and lint, plus a QA judge — and a failed gate hands the agent the real log to go again.

route

Frontier money only where it earns it.

Most of an ask is not hard. The router splits it into jobs and binds each to the model the benchmark measured best on that work — usually not the expensive one. That is where the numbers above come from.

learn

It learns your repo.

Every run is scored on your machine — which merge, which job, how long, what it cost, whether it passed first time — and filed in a benchmark that grows as you use it. The numbers above are a starting point; the ones the router obeys are yours. Nothing leaves the machine.

Adoption is finished and control never arrived: 90 % of professional developers use coding agents weekly and 68 % daily, but only 29 % trust what comes out — down from 40 %, with 3 % who highly trust it and seven in ten refusing to merge without reading it themselves. Cowork

Cheaper — one agent that takes the role the ask needs, and the models under it are merged.

  1. 01

    One agent that changes role.

    Not a roster to staff. You ask, and the agent takes the role the ask needs — Backender, Frontend designer, Tester, Reviewer — arriving with the merge the benchmark picked for that kind of work, plus its globs, skills and specialists. The router picks the role per ask; one switch turns role routing off if you would rather it did not. One dial, three positions: cheaper, balanced, faster.

  2. 02

    The conversation is the workspace.

    Not a thread, not a box you compose first. One conversation carries the role it is in, its scope, its skills and its specialists — and the two models it is merged from, named in the header as it works. Switching role does not restart anything: only changing provider does.

  3. 03

    A tool opens beside the conversation.

    A diff, a board, the code graph, the design frames — one click, and it takes half the screen next to the agent. One at a time, so there is no layout to build. A canvas takes the whole desk. What you put in a board is still there next week.

  4. 04

    Heimdall routes every ask — and checks the claim.

    The router reads the ask first and binds a model to every job of it — planning, editing, verifying, judging — from the models actually installed on your machine. The dials set context and effort; AUTO decides both. In the same menu, three switches: run the project's own test, build and lint the moment an agent says it is done. A gate that fails hands the agent the real log and it goes again — once, not forever.

  5. 05

    Episodes, and a report at the end of every turn.

    A long conversation is not one wall of scrollback. Close an episode and it consolidates into a summary and the story of what happened; ! brings a closed one back as context. And every turn closes with what it actually did — who decided and who wrote, each stage in plain English, every gate labelled a machine's or a model's, the diff beside the old version of each file, and the count: files, tools, tokens, the clock, and what the router did not spend.

X-ray

Safety — every file judged, every number explained, before anything ships.

  1. 01

    The verdict, not a vibe.

    Every file reviewed and scored — and why not 100, axis by axis: size, complexity, coupling (fan-in and fan-out), density, churn and untested. Arithmetic over your ASTs and your git history; no model is asked for an opinion and the same commit scores the same twice.

  2. 02

    Laws, not lint.

    The rules your codebase lives by, checked at the line: what breaks a law, where, and what compliance is worth. A senior review that reads the same way every time.

  3. 03

    The codebase as a map.

    Every file a box, area its mass, colour its health; hubs, orphans, cycles, the most central and the most risky — at a glance, and per folder.

  4. 04

    What the tests never reach. What a change would touch.

    Coverage gaps by file, the blast radius before a change lands, and the review over the snapshots — the score's history, scrubbable.

  5. 05

    The same intel feeds the agent.

    Reviews, graph, docs and ASTs become the context pack every ask starts from — what MIN mode navigates by. Design too: your screens captured from the running app, the design system and its drift.

What is antiloki, in one sentence?

A desktop workspace that merges the model subscriptions you already have into one coding agent — the model measured best for each job of an ask, not one model for all of them. It makes your weekly limit last longer and each ask cost less, with folder-level write enforcement, per-task verification and a full audit timeline: the control layer the agents don't ship with.

Does it replace Claude Code, Codex or OpenCode?

No — it runs them, twice over: as the agent in its box, and as the engine behind antiloki's own AI (the chat, the reviews, the ultra review). Keep your subscriptions; there is no API key to paste.

What does the X-ray do that a linter doesn't?

It judges every file against your rules and its own measurements (tests, coupling, structure, change) and tells you why a file is not 100. Then the ultra review: a $0 survey of the territory, and your engine reading the worst units to report what it can verify — with the fix. The chat sees all of it, so "why is this file 81?" gets a real answer.

Do I need two subscriptions?

For a merge, two — and the cheap half can be free. A merge never puts two frontier models together; it puts cheap hands under a good brain. antiloki only offers a provider you are actually signed in to: it reads the seat, not the installed binary, so a CLI sitting on your machine unauthenticated is never offered as something it can bind. With one provider it runs solo — still routed per job, still audited, just not merged.

Isn't this just worktrees and tmux?

Worktrees are the easy part. The write gate at the tool call, the verifier before merge and the cost-per-task record are what you can't script in an afternoon — and what the trial is for.

More calls must mean a bigger bill.

More calls, less money: the expensive model is asked only for the part that needs it, and the cheap one does the rest. That is the whole trade, and it is why the bill is on screen for every turn — including the runs where the router declined to split because splitting was not worth it. A failed run reports what it spent and claims no saving.

Can my company install it?

One binary, local, no account, no telemetry. The complete list of network calls is: your CLIs' own calls, a daily license check, an update check against a static file. Send that to security.
